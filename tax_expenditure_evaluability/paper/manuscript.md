## Abstract

Tax expenditure reports are meant to make spending through the tax system visible, but measuring revenue forgone is not the same as making a tax expenditure evaluable. This article assesses how far Türkiye's official reporting allows evaluation. It analyses nine official Tax Expenditure Reports (2016–2025), from which 2,112 annex items (10,560 item-year estimates) and 683 narrative sections were extracted and reconciled with published totals. Nine criteria derived from the IMF's guidance on evaluating tax expenditures were then coded at item and section level. Cost reporting improved: the share of applicable items with an item-level estimate rose from 76% to 88%. Every estimate carries a quality label ("very good", "good", "approximate"), but the reports never define these labels or quantify uncertainty. Narratives usually identify eligible beneficiaries and often give a purpose, but purposes are frequently generic, outcomes are never quantified, and no report publishes beneficiary counts, outcome indicators, counterfactual or additionality analysis, cost-effectiveness comparisons or review results. Projections and budget-law estimates are revised substantially by later estimates. The reports therefore support cost monitoring but not evaluation. The findings concern the information in the reports, not the effectiveness of the measures.

**Keywords:** tax expenditures; evaluability; fiscal transparency; revenue forgone; IMF; Türkiye

**JEL codes:** H20, H25, H61, H83

## 1. Introduction

Governments spend through the tax system as well as through appropriations. Exemptions, deductions, reduced rates and deferrals forgo revenue in pursuit of economic or social objectives. Since @surrey1973 and @surrey1985 framed these provisions as spending programmes delivered through the tax code, the main policy response has been reporting: estimating the revenue forgone by each provision and publishing the list alongside the budget. More than one hundred countries now publish some tax expenditure information [@beer2022, p. 1].

Reporting costs is, however, only the starting point. The IMF's How-To Note on evaluating tax expenditures [@beer2022] states that greater transparency about revenue impact "does not by itself ensure that the funds are being well spent" (p. 1). It sets out what an evaluation needs: an explicit objective, identified beneficiaries, reliable cost estimates, a theory of change with indicators defined in advance, an assessment against a counterfactual, a comparison with alternative instruments, and a periodic evaluation cycle (pp. 3–7). Whether a tax expenditure *can* be evaluated therefore depends on information that goes well beyond its cost estimate. The evaluation literature calls this property evaluability [@leviton2010; @trevisan2007].

This article asks how evaluable Türkiye's tax expenditures are on the basis of official reporting. Türkiye is an informative case for three reasons. First, its tax expenditures are large: the latest report estimates them at 6.17% of GDP and 37.7% of general budget tax revenue for 2024 (Table 2). Second, it has a legal reporting mandate (Law No. 5018, Art. 15) and a long reporting history, with an annual report series since 2016. Third, international comparisons record Türkiye as estimating most of its identified tax expenditures while not referring to evaluations in its reporting [@beer2022, Table 1, p. 3]. That comparison is a single snapshot. A provision-level analysis of the reports over time has been missing.

The research question is: *What information do Türkiye's official tax expenditure reports provide on the cost, objectives, beneficiaries and policy outcomes of individual tax expenditures, and to what extent does it meet the information requirements of the IMF evaluation framework?* The question concerns the evaluability of reported information. The study does not estimate, and does not claim to estimate, the economic or social effects of any tax expenditure. That a report contains no outcome information is a finding about the report, not evidence that a measure is ineffective.

The study makes three contributions. First, it builds a reproducible provision-level dataset from nine official reports (2016–2025), with every number traceable to a page of a source document and reconciled against the reports' own totals. Second, it operationalises the IMF guidance into nine criteria and codes them at the level of individual tax expenditures, with a public codebook, decision log and evidence sentences. Third, it documents features of the reporting that summary indicators miss: the undefined quality labels, size of revisions between vintages, provisions that are never costed, and changes in narrative content over time.

The remainder of the article is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the concept of tax expenditures, their measurement and the link between reporting, evaluation and accountability. Section 3 describes Türkiye's reporting system. Section 4 presents the data and coding method. Section 5 reports the findings. Section 6 discusses them against the IMF framework, and Section 7 concludes. Limitations are set out in Section 8.

## 2. Conceptual framework and related literature

### 2.1 Tax expenditures as spending through the tax system

A tax expenditure is a departure from a benchmark tax system that gives preferential treatment to particular taxpayers, activities or regions [@surrey1985; @burton2013; @beer2022, Box 1]. The concept rests on an analogy with direct spending. A deduction for an activity has budgetary effects similar to a grant for the same activity, but it is less visible and usually escapes the annual scrutiny that appropriations receive [@surrey1973; @toder2000]. Political-science work describes the resulting "hidden" or "submerged" state, in which a substantial part of social and economic policy is delivered through the tax code with weak visibility to citizens and legislators [@howard1999; @mettler2011].

The concept is contested at its foundation, because the benchmark is a normative choice. Whether a provision is a tax expenditure or part of the "normal" tax structure depends on that choice. @burman2003 shows that this ambiguity has been used to question the concept's usefulness, and argues that the concept remains relevant as a tool for scrutinising spending-like provisions. Reported totals are therefore not neutral quantities: they reflect benchmark decisions that differ across countries and can change over time [@oecd2010; @burton2013].

### 2.2 Measuring the cost of tax expenditures

Three costing methods are standard. The revenue-forgone method estimates the revenue lost under current behaviour. The revenue-gain method estimates what repeal would yield after behavioural responses. The outlay-equivalence method estimates the direct spending needed to provide the same benefit [@altshuler2011; @burton2013; @oecd2010]. Most countries use revenue forgone. It is the simplest method, but its estimates are not additive across provisions and do not measure the revenue that abolition would raise [@altshuler2011]. Item-level estimates are also often missing for provisions where administrative data are unavailable. Published totals are therefore lower bounds of uneven quality [@beer2022, p. 1, fn. 1; @brixi2003]. Estimates of the size and distribution of tax expenditures [@burman2008] show why they matter for fiscal and distributional analysis. They also show that the cost figures, however computed, describe the size of support rather than its effects.

### 2.3 From reporting to evaluation

Reporting answers the question "how much?". Evaluation asks whether a tax expenditure achieves its objective, and at what direct and indirect cost [@beer2022, p. 5]. The IMF note sets out what such an evaluation needs. Its objectives and intended beneficiaries must be understood. A theory of change should map how the incentive alters behaviour and outcomes (p. 6). Indicators and data sources should be defined *ex ante* (p. 5). Effectiveness should be judged against a counterfactual that addresses additionality, redundancy and displacement (pp. 5, 7). Costs should include the reliability of the revenue estimate (p. 7). The measure should be compared with alternative instruments such as direct outlays (pp. 5–6). Finally, all tax expenditures should be evaluated periodically within a multi-year cycle, and results should be published (pp. 3–4, 12). The note cites evidence that for almost 70% of the tax expenditures in the Global Tax Expenditures Database the revenue estimate is not linked to a stated policy objective (fn. 10, p. 5). The note is institutional guidance, not a peer-reviewed standard, and it deliberately offers no scored checklist; any set of criteria derived from it is an operationalisation.

The economic literature on investment incentives illustrates why the counterfactual matters. Incentives may subsidise investment that would have occurred anyway and may relocate rather than create activity. The evidence on their effects is mixed and context-dependent [@zee2002; @vanparys2010; @klemm2012]. @redonda2019 characterise tax expenditures as largely opaque, costly and often ineffective in reaching their stated goals, and call for more transparent reporting and better design. Temporary provisions and sunset clauses are often proposed as review mechanisms, but whether temporariness leads to actual review is an empirical question [@barsimantov2018].

### 2.4 Transparency, accountability and evaluability

Transparency supports accountability only when the information disclosed can be used to judge performance. @heald2003 distinguishes transparency about events (what was spent) from transparency about processes (how decisions are made and assessed). Cost reports provide event transparency. Evaluation requires process information: objectives, performance indicators and reviews. Evaluability assessment, developed in programme evaluation, asks *before* an evaluation whether a programme has defined objectives, plausible logic and available data. Document review is one of its standard methods [@trevisan2007; @leviton2010]. This article applies that logic to tax expenditure reports: it treats the official report as the document and asks whether it contains the information an evaluator would need.

### 2.5 Research on Türkiye

Turkish research on tax expenditures has mainly described their size and composition using the official reports. @ericok2019 reviews the concept and the estimates for 2015–2021, compares TE-to-GDP ratios across countries, and stresses efficiency and fiscal transparency (summary based on the published abstract). @yoruldu2021 analyses the 2019–2023 tax expenditures in terms of the functions of taxation using document analysis (abstract). @doganalp2020 argues that budget discipline requires weighing the fiscal cost of tax expenditures against their benefits (abstract only). @isikman2024 compute deviations between realised and projected tax expenditures in the reports for 2015–2018 (abstract). These studies use the reported figures. None of them examines the reports as evaluation instruments, which is the gap addressed here. Comparative work on transparency [@dziemianowicz2017] and on the politics of tax expenditures [@haldenwang2021] places such reporting in a wider fiscal-governance context.

## 3. Tax expenditure reporting in Türkiye

Law No. 5018 on Public Financial Management and Control requires that tax expenditures be included in the annual budget law. The 2025 report states that the provision aims to provide information on the budgetary cost of tax expenditures and "on those who benefit from them" (VHR2025, PDF p. 23). After a first report in 2001 and a report under a new methodology in 2007, tax expenditure amounts were published only as budget-law annexes between 2008 and 2015. An annual report has been published since 2016 (VHR2025, PDF pp. 12–13). The 2016–2018 reports were issued by the Ministry of Finance (from 2018, the Ministry of Treasury and Finance) and the 2020–2025 reports by the Revenue Administration (Table 1). No 2019 report could be found in either institution's archive.

The reports share a common structure. An introductory chapter defines tax expenditures and the method. A provisions chapter gives one narrative section for each article or group of articles: the statute text, a statement of rationale, the reasons for classifying the provision as a tax expenditure, and the intended method of calculation. A summary chapter presents totals and ratios to GDP and to tax revenue. Annexes give item-level amounts (Annex I), a functional classification (Annex II), the five largest items (Annex III) and their sectoral distribution (Annex IV). Each annex row reports one *realisation* year and four *projection* years, together with an estimate-quality label.

Three features of the system matter for evaluability. First, all estimates are static revenue-forgone figures computed "on the assumption that the tax expenditure does not cause a change in taxpayer behaviour or economic activity" (VHR2025, PDF p. 23). Second, the "realisation" column is itself an estimate built from returns and administrative data for the latest available year. Projections scale these amounts with the macroeconomic targets of the Medium-Term Programme and with known legal changes. The 2016 report states that projections grow in line with nominal GDP, so that their ratio to GDP stays constant (VHR2016, PDF p. 290). Third, defence-related exemptions and provisions arising from international agreements are excluded by definition (VHR2025, PDF p. 23).

The series contains a methodological break. The 2016 report prints both the old budget-annex series based on the 2007 method (1.34% of GDP for 2015) and the "new determination" (4.08% of GDP for 2015; VHR2016, PDF pp. 284–290). The two cannot be combined, so the analysis starts in 2016. Coverage also changed. Only the 2016 report lists provisions of stamp duty, fees, inheritance, property and municipal taxes (231 provisions), and it does so without cost estimates. Later reports cover seven taxes: personal income tax (GV), corporate tax (KV), VAT (KDV), special consumption tax (ÖTV), banking and insurance transactions tax (BSMV), motor vehicle tax (MTV) and special communication tax (ÖİV). The headline figure also changed meaning. The 2018–2022 reports emphasise the realisation year, whereas the 2023–2025 reports emphasise a projection for the publication year. For example, the 2025 report headlines 5.1% of GDP for 2025, while its realisation estimate for 2024 is 6.17%.

## 4. Data and methodology

### 4.1 Documents

Permanent URLs, file metadata and local copies are recorded in the source inventory (`data/source_inventory.csv`). Budget-law estimates are taken from the tables that each report reprints, and they are kept as a separate series to avoid double counting. The IMF How-To Note 22/05 was read in full.

### 4.2 Extraction and reconciliation

Annex I tables were extracted programmatically from the PDF files. The 2020–2023 tables are printed rotated, the 2017 report uses a broken character encoding, and the 2018 annex uses fonts without Unicode maps; the last was decoded with a glyph map learned from the other reports. Each annex row yields the provision's legal reference, description, five annual amounts with a status code for each cell, and the quality label. Cell statuses distinguish numerical estimates from the reports' standard phrases: "data could not be obtained" (*veri temin edilememiştir*), "no tax expenditure arises" (*vergi harcaması oluşmamıştır*), "new measure" (*yeni uygulama*), "included under another item", and dashes.

The result is 2,112 report-item rows and 10,560 report-item-year observations (`data/tax_expenditure_panel.csv`). Two reconciliations were carried out. First, item rows were summed and compared with each sub-table's printed total. They agree within ±5 thousand TL in 395 of 402 comparisons. Second, annex totals were compared with the summary tables in the body of each report. They agree within ±0.5 million TL for all reports that publish such tables. Every remaining discrepancy was traced to the documents themselves (`outputs/tables/official_document_inconsistencies.csv`). In the 2023 report, for example, the printed total omits a listed item. In the 2022 report, a sub-table total includes an amount for a year in which the item itself shows a dash. Items were linked across reports by legal reference and description similarity, yielding 338 distinct items. These links are an analytical construct, and comparisons over time use only links based on both legal reference and description.

Aggregate ratios to GDP and to tax revenue are the ratios published in each report; no denominators are recomputed. Because inflation was high during the period, nominal TL amounts are not compared across years. Amount-weighted shares are computed within a single report and year only.

### 4.3 Narrative sections and the coding frame

Narrative sections were extracted with page markers (74 sections in 2016, 22 in 2017, 76 in 2018 and 82–87 in 2020–2025; in every case the sections match the report's table of contents). The 2017 report narrates only new or amended provisions.

Table A1 summarises the nine criteria, which operationalise the IMF note's information requirements. Codes take the values 2 (explicit and provision-specific), 1 (partial, generic, shared with other provisions or implicit), 0 (absent from the document) and NA (not applicable). C1 (policy objective), C2 (beneficiaries), C5 (expected outcome), C6 (indicator or outcome data), C7 (additionality or counterfactual), C8 (cost-effectiveness or alternative instruments) and C9 (regular review, sunset clause or renewal evaluation) are coded per narrative section. C3 (item-level cost estimate) and C4 (estimate quality or uncertainty) are coded per item-year from the annex. Two auxiliary variables record whether the estimation method is described (M) and whether the number of beneficiaries is reported (B). The full decision rules are in the codebook (`data/codebook.md`).

Coding proceeded in two layers. First, explicit rules derived from the codebook were applied to every section of every report. The rules separate the report's own narrative from quoted statute text, use cue lists, filter known false cues (for example, purpose words inside the titles of laws) and record, for each code, the evidence sentence and its PDF page. Second, the author reviewed the results. Every C1 and C5 evidence sentence in the 2016 and 2025 reports was read. Every section coded C1 = 0 in those reports was re-read for missed purpose statements. Every rule hit on C6–C8, B and C9 = 2 in any report was examined; all C6, C7 and B hits, and all C9 = 2 hits, were false positives. Every code that changed between consecutive reports for the same section was checked against both texts. Corrections were logged with a decision identifier (`data/coding/overrides.csv`, `data/coding/decision_log.md`). There was no independent second coder, so no inter-coder reliability statistic is reported. The rule layer makes the coding reproducible and auditable, but it does not replace independent double coding.

Narrative codes were attached to annex items through their legal references. In 2020–2025, 74–84% of items map to a narrative section (Table 1). Items without a narrative section, including all BSMV, MTV and ÖİV items, are coded 0 on the narrative criteria. Where one section covers many sub-provisions, a code of 2 can rest on a purpose stated for only one of them, so item-level narrative codes are upper bounds.

## 5. Findings

### 5.1 Scale and the revenue-forgone figures

Measured against GDP, reported tax expenditures rose from about 4.1% in the realisation years 2015, 2017 and 2019 to 5.85% in 2021 and a peak of 6.52% in 2023, then fell to 6.17% in 2024 (Table 2, Figure 1). Relative to general budget tax revenue, they rose from 19.5% in 2015 to 37–38% in 2022–2024. These are revenue-forgone figures computed under the reports' benchmark. They measure the size of support delivered through the tax system and say nothing about what that support achieves.

[Table 2 and Figure 1 about here]

Figure 1 also shows that each report's projections lie below the next realisation estimate. Table 3 compares estimates for the same fiscal year from different report vintages; because these are same-year comparisons, nominal amounts can be compared directly. Realisation estimates exceeded the first projection for the same year by 5.7% to 35.7% in every comparison available. They exceeded the budget-law estimates printed two years earlier by 24.5% (2024), 73.9% (2023), 83.7% (2021) and 162.1% (2022). The ratio to GDP shows the same pattern; for 2021 the projection was 4.7% and the realisation 5.85%. At item level the dispersion is larger (Table 3b). Among strongly linked items, only 10–24% of realisation estimates fall within ±10% of the earlier projection, and 30–46% differ from it by more than half. These revisions combine macroeconomic forecast errors, inflation, legal changes and updates to data and methods, and the reports do not decompose them. The budget-law figures that legislators see therefore give an uncertain guide to the eventual estimated cost.

### 5.2 Cost information at item level (C3)

Item-level cost reporting improved over the period (Table 4, Figure 2). The share of applicable items with a numerical realisation estimate rose from 75.5% in 2016 to 88.1% in 2024 and 2025. Gaps remain. Each report contains 22–35 items marked "data could not be obtained", and the 2016–2018 reports also contain 10–16 "new measure" items without estimates. Among the 284 items that appear in three or more reports, 25 are never estimated in any report and 85 lack an estimate in at least one (Table 10). Unestimated provisions in the latest report include, for example, the Istanbul Finance Centre exemptions, several corporate tax exemptions for public and village enterprises, and income tax exemptions for trade-fair income. Item-level costs are thus available for most, but not all, provisions, and the missing ones are largely the same from year to year.

### 5.3 Quality of estimates (C4)

Almost every estimated item carries a quality label: "very good" (*çok iyi*), "good" (*iyi*) or "approximate" (*yaklaşık*) (98–100% of estimated items; Table 5). The only definition offered, in the 2016, 2018 and 2020–2025 reports, is a single sentence: the labels classify estimates "according to the reliability and coverage of the data used" (e.g. VHR2025, PDF p. 15). The 2017 report gives no definition. No report states the criteria for assigning a label, and none publishes ranges, standard errors or sensitivity analyses. Every labelled item is therefore coded C4 = 1 and none reaches 2.

The labels have nonetheless shifted substantially. In 2016–2020, items labelled "very good" accounted for 58–62% of the estimated amount. In 2025 the share is 14%, and "approximate" items account for 42.5% (Table 5, Figure 3). The change is driven by large items. The largest single item in the 2025 report, the exemption of the minimum wage from income tax, accounts for 23.5% of the estimated total and is labelled "approximate" (Table 9). The share of the cost figure that the reports themselves classify as approximate has therefore grown, while the meaning of the classification remains undefined.

### 5.4 Objectives and beneficiaries (C1, C2)

Most narrative sections give some rationale for the provision. In 2025, 51% of applicable sections contain a provision-specific statement of purpose (for example, reducing transport costs to encourage exports, or reducing dependence on imported energy and protecting the environment), and 23% contain only a generic one (Table 7). The commonest generic formula, "social and economic purposes are pursued together" (*sosyal ve ekonomik amaç bir arada*), appears in 26 of the 84 sections of the 2025 report and in 25 of 74 sections in 2016. Some sections give a purpose that belongs to an amendment rather than to the tax expenditure itself. In 2025, for example, the section on the exemption for simplified-method taxpayers explains why the exemption was narrowed, not why it exists. Purposes can also disappear. The section on deductions for firms providing services abroad (GVK Art. 33) contained a purpose statement up to 2021, but the shortened text used from 2022 onward does not.

At item level, which includes items without any narrative, 48% of items in the 2025 report have a provision-specific purpose, 29% a generic one and 23% none (Table 6, Figure 4). Weighted by estimated cost, items with a specific purpose account for 64.8% of the 2024 total (Table 8). Beneficiary groups are almost always defined, usually through the quoted statute's eligibility conditions (96–97% of sections). No section, however, reports how many taxpayers benefit (B = 0 throughout). This is notable because the 2025 report itself presents Law 5018's reporting requirement as a means of obtaining information on beneficiaries (PDF p. 23).

### 5.5 Outcomes, indicators, additionality and alternatives (C5–C8)

The reports describe what the provisions are meant to change in general terms, but never in measurable terms. About two in five applicable sections name an outcome, such as exports, employment, investment, R&D, energy dependence or financial-market depth (37% in 2025; 43–48% in 2016 and 2018). No section quantifies the outcome, links it to a target or names an indicator, so C5 never reaches 2 (Tables 6 and 7).

The remaining evaluation criteria are absent. No report publishes non-cost data on the use or results of any provision, such as the number of claimants, investment or jobs associated with an incentive, or any other performance indicator (C6 = 0 for all items and sections). No report discusses whether the supported activity would have occurred without the tax expenditure (C7 = 0). Apart from one sentence in the 2016–2021 sections on the minimum living allowance (*asgari geçim indirimi*), which describes the measure as support for wage earners "instead of direct public spending", no report compares a tax expenditure with an alternative instrument or relates its cost to its results (C8 ≤ 1). These results hold in every report from 2016 to 2025 and for every item weighted by cost (Table 8). The most expensive items illustrate this (Table 9). The ten largest items, together 62.8% of the 2024 estimated total, include the minimum wage exemption, the exemption for investment funds, the reduced corporate tax rate for investments under incentive certificates and the VAT exemption for feed and fertiliser. Several of them have stated purposes, but none comes with any indicator, counterfactual or efficiency information.

### 5.6 Review and time limits (C9)

About half of the narrative sections reproduce statute text containing a time limit or describe a provision as temporary (49–54% of sections; C9 = 1). Typical examples are an exemption that applies "until 31/12/2028" or an incentive available for a set number of tax periods. No section reports a scheduled review, an evaluation feeding into a decision to extend or repeal a provision, or the results of any review (C9 = 2 never occurs). Extensions and repeals appear in the narratives as legal facts without evaluative reasoning. This contrasts with the 2016 report's own account of why tax expenditures are monitored: reporting is said to make it possible to establish "whether the purpose of the regulation is still valid" and to identify "efficient and inefficient applications" (VHR2016, PDF p. 26). The reports do not carry out this function.

### 5.7 Methods and coverage of the narratives

The narratives are more informative about *how the cost should be computed* than about *what the provision achieves*. In 2025, 51% of sections describe a provision-specific computation with a data source, typically the relevant return line taxed at the statutory or effective rate (M = 2). Another 41% describe a computation without a data source (Table 7). The reports caution that the ideal method could not always be used because of data limitations, and that the "best applicable method" was used instead (VHR2025, PDF p. 303). The narratives therefore describe intended computations, not necessarily the ones actually performed. The provisions in the annex with the weakest narrative coverage are those in the BSMV, MTV and ÖİV tables and in the "other laws" sub-tables, which together account for 9.1% of the estimated 2024 amount (Section 4.3).

## 6. Discussion in relation to the IMF evaluation framework

Mapped onto the IMF framework, Türkiye's reporting covers the first steps of the evaluation sequence and stops before the evaluative ones. The note's first step, "understand how a TE works, whom it benefits, and its intended purpose" [@beer2022, p. 5], is partly supported. The legal mechanics and eligible groups are reported systematically, purposes are reported for most of the cost, and the estimation logic is described. The cost element is reported more fully than in many countries, as an item-level revenue-forgone estimate for nearly nine-tenths of applicable provisions. Neither element is complete. Purposes are frequently generic and are not stated in a way that could be tested. The note asks "how large and how reliable" costs are (p. 7), but reliability is communicated only through labels whose criteria are not disclosed, and the size of revisions between vintages is never reported.

The evaluative core of the framework finds no counterpart in the reports. This includes the theory of change and indicators defined in advance (pp. 5–6), the counterfactual assessment of effectiveness including additionality and displacement (pp. 5, 7), the comparison with direct outlays (pp. 5–6), and the periodic evaluation cycle (pp. 3–4). The pattern has persisted over ten years of reporting, three issuing institutions' practices and growing tax expenditures. The IMF's 2017 snapshot recorded that Türkiye's reporting did not refer to evaluations [@beer2022, Table 1, p. 3]. The provision-level analysis here shows that this remained the case through the 2025 report, and that it applies to the largest and fastest-growing items as well as to marginal ones.

Three qualifications are needed. First, absence from the reports does not establish absence from government. Evaluations, monitoring data and audits may exist elsewhere, for example in ministries that administer incentive certificates. Their non-appearance in the official tax expenditure report means, however, that they are not part of the information presented to Parliament with the budget. Second, the IMF note itself recommends a pragmatic, triaged approach and accepts that many evaluations will be qualitative [@beer2022, pp. 4, 12]. The reports' narratives could provide a natural vehicle for this. They already describe each provision's mechanics. Adding a stated objective, a small set of indicators and a review date would make them the backbone of an evaluation cycle. Third, the revenue-forgone figures should not be over-interpreted. They measure gross support under fixed behaviour [@altshuler2011]. The substantial revisions documented here mean that the budget-law figures are a weak anchor for fiscal decisions. This is a problem of reliability, which the IMF framework treats as part of cost assessment, and it is separate from the question of effectiveness.

The findings also bear on the transparency–accountability link [@heald2003]. Türkiye's reporting provides substantial event transparency: what is forgone, on which provisions, by tax type. It provides little process transparency. The reports do not show how objectives are set, how performance is judged or how decisions to renew or repeal are reached. Under such conditions, as @beer2022 warn (p. 1), debate on the benefits of tax expenditures risks being shaped mainly by their beneficiaries. The submerged character of tax-based policy [@howard1999; @mettler2011] is thus only partly resolved by cost reporting.

## 7. Conclusion and policy implications

Türkiye publishes detailed, item-level tax expenditure reports whose internal accounts largely reconcile and whose coverage of costed items has improved over time. Measured against the information requirements of the IMF evaluation framework, the reports make tax expenditures *visible* but not *evaluable*. Costs are reported. Purposes and beneficiary groups are usually described. Outcome indicators, beneficiary counts, counterfactual reasoning, efficiency comparisons and review results are not reported for any provision in any year. The reliability of the cost figures is signalled through undefined labels, and the figures are revised substantially between vintages.

The analysis suggests several low-cost changes to the reports themselves, consistent with the incremental approach of @beer2022:

1. Define the estimate-quality labels with explicit criteria and publish, for at least the largest items, a range or the revision relative to the previous vintage.
2. Publish, alongside the budget-law estimate, the eventual realisation estimate for past years, so that systematic under-estimation becomes visible.
3. For each provision, state a specific objective, the expected outcome and at least one indicator, together with the data source and the responsible institution.
4. Report the number of beneficiaries where returns already identify them. Many computation descriptions already rely on specific return lines.
5. Attach a review date to each provision. Begin with the ten largest items, which account for 63% of the estimated cost, and publish review findings in the report.
6. Restore narrative coverage for BSMV, MTV, ÖİV and "other law" provisions, and explain changes in coverage and labels between reports.

These recommendations concern information. Whether any particular tax expenditure is effective is a separate question, which can be answered only once this information exists.

## 8. Limitations

The analysis covers the official tax expenditure reports only. The 2019 report could not be found, and budget-law annexes were not retrieved separately. Statistics from the Ministry of Industry and Technology could not be accessed automatically. In any case, they report planned investment and employment and would not measure costs or effects. Extraction from PDFs required decoding and, for five 2016 rows, manual transcription; reconciliation against printed totals limits but cannot exclude extraction errors. Coding was done by a single author using explicit rules and recorded evidence, without an independent second coder. Section-level narrative codes attributed to items are upper bounds for multi-provision sections. Time limits (C9 = 1) are mostly read from quoted statute and do not imply that any review takes place. Revenue-forgone estimates assume no behavioural response and are not measures of policy effects or of revenue from repeal. Revisions between vintages are not decomposed into their causes. A fuller list is provided in `paper/limitations_and_data_gaps.md`.
