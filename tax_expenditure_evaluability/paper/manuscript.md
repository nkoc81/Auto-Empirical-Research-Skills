## Abstract

Tax expenditure reports make spending through the tax system visible, but a cost estimate alone does not make a tax expenditure evaluable. This article examines how far Türkiye's published tax expenditure reporting supports evaluation, using the information requirements set out in the IMF's guidance on evaluating tax expenditures. It analyses nine official Tax Expenditure Reports (2016–2025) and the tax expenditure annexes of the 2019 and 2020 budget laws. From the reports, 2,112 report-item rows (10,560 item-year estimates) and 683 narrative sections were extracted and reconciled with published totals. Nine criteria, the author's operationalisation of the IMF guidance, were coded by a single coder. Most items carry an item-level revenue-forgone estimate (79–88% of applicable items in the 2020–2025 reports) together with a quality label that the reports do not define. Later estimates for the same year differ substantially from earlier projections and budget-law figures. Narratives usually define eligibility, and provide a stated purpose for many provisions. No report provides quantified expected outcomes, beneficiary counts, outcome indicators, counterfactual analysis, cost-effectiveness comparisons or review results. The published reporting therefore supports cost monitoring but does not allow an assessment of effectiveness. The study does not assess the effectiveness of any measure.

**Keywords:** tax expenditures; evaluability; fiscal transparency; revenue forgone; IMF; Türkiye

**JEL codes:** H20, H25, H61, H83

## 1. Introduction

Governments spend through the tax system as well as through appropriations. Exemptions, deductions, reduced rates and deferrals forgo revenue in pursuit of economic or social objectives. The tax expenditure concept, which treats such provisions as spending delivered through the tax code, is generally traced to the work of Stanley Surrey [@surrey1973; @surrey1985]. The main policy response has been reporting: estimating the revenue forgone by each provision and publishing the estimates. According to the IMF, 102 countries publish some information on the cost of tax expenditures [@beer2022, p. 1].

Reporting costs is only a first step. The IMF's How-To Note on evaluating tax expenditures states that greater transparency about revenue impact "does not by itself ensure that the funds are being well spent" [@beer2022, p. 1]. The note describes what an evaluation requires: an understanding of a measure's purpose and intended beneficiaries, reliable cost estimates, a theory of change with indicators defined in advance, an assessment against a counterfactual, a comparison with alternative instruments, and a periodic evaluation cycle with published results (pp. 3–7, 11). Whether a tax expenditure can be evaluated therefore depends on information beyond its cost estimate. Programme evaluation calls this property evaluability [@leviton2010; @trevisan2007].

This article asks how far Türkiye's official tax expenditure reporting supports evaluation. Türkiye is an informative case. Its reported tax expenditures are large: the latest report estimates them at 6.17% of GDP and 37.68% of general budget tax revenue for 2024 (Table 2). It has a legal reporting mandate (Law No. 5018, Art. 15) and an annual report series since 2016. In a cross-country table compiled in the IMF note, Türkiye's 2017 reporting is recorded as identifying 222 tax expenditures, estimating 183, and not referring to evaluation [@beer2022, Table 1, p. 3]. That table is a single snapshot; a provision-level analysis of the reports over time has not been available.

The research question is: *What information do Türkiye's official tax expenditure reports provide on the cost, objectives, beneficiaries and expected outcomes of individual tax expenditures, and to what extent does this information meet the information requirements of the IMF evaluation framework?* The question concerns the published reporting. The study does not estimate the economic or social effects of any tax expenditure. It also does not examine whether evaluations, monitoring data or audits exist elsewhere in government. That a report contains no outcome information is a finding about the report, not evidence that a measure is ineffective or that no assessment is carried out.

The study makes three contributions. First, it builds a reproducible provision-level dataset from the official reports, in which every number is traceable to a page of a source document and the extracted values are reconciled against the reports' own totals. Second, it operationalises the IMF guidance into nine criteria and codes them for narrative sections and annex items, with a codebook, a decision log and an evidence sentence for every code. Third, it documents features of the reporting that summary indicators miss: undefined quality labels, revisions between estimate vintages, provisions that are never costed, and changes in narrative content.

Section 2 reviews the literature. Section 3 describes Türkiye's reporting system. Section 4 presents the data and coding method. Section 5 reports the findings, Section 6 discusses them against the IMF framework, and Section 7 concludes. Section 8 sets out the limitations.

## 2. Conceptual framework and related literature

### 2.1 Tax expenditures as spending through the tax system

A tax expenditure is a departure from a benchmark tax system that gives preferential treatment to particular taxpayers, activities or regions [@beer2022, Box 1, p. 2; @burton2013]. The concept rests on an analogy with direct spending: a tax relief and a grant for the same activity both reduce the resources available for other purposes [@beer2022, p. 1]. Because tax reliefs do not pass through annual appropriations, a large literature, including work on the "hidden" or "submerged" character of policy delivered through the tax code, has been concerned with their visibility to citizens and legislators [@howard1999; @mettler2011].

The benchmark is a normative choice, so the boundary between a tax expenditure and the "normal" tax structure is contested [@burman2003; @burton2013]. Reported totals therefore reflect benchmark decisions that differ across countries and can change over time [@oecd2010; @beer2022, p. 1, fn. 1].

### 2.2 Measuring the cost of tax expenditures

The standard costing approaches are revenue forgone, revenue gain and outlay equivalence [@altshuler2011; @oecd2010]. The Turkish reports describe the same three approaches and state that they use revenue forgone (VHR2025, PDF pp. 22–23). Revenue-forgone estimates hold taxpayer behaviour fixed, so they are not estimates of the revenue that repeal would raise [@altshuler2011]. Published totals are often incomplete because provisions without data are not costed [@beer2022, p. 1, fn. 1; @brixi2003]. The cost figures describe the size of support, not its effects.

### 2.3 From reporting to evaluation

Reporting answers the question of how much revenue is forgone. Evaluation asks whether a tax expenditure achieves its stated or implied objective, and at what direct and indirect cost [@beer2022, p. 5]. The IMF note's guidance can be summarised in its own terms. The first step is "to understand how a TE works, whom it benefits, and its intended purpose" (p. 5). Indicators and data sources for future assessments should be defined *ex ante* (p. 5). A theory of change should map how the incentive alters behaviour and outcomes (p. 6). Effectiveness should be assessed against a counterfactual, including whether the desired behaviour would occur anyway and the potential for displacement (pp. 5, 7). Cost assessment includes the question "How large and how reliable are the cost estimates for the program?" (Box 2, p. 7). Tax expenditures may be compared with direct outlays (p. 6). All tax expenditures should be evaluated periodically within a multi-year cycle (pp. 3–4), and evaluation reports should be published (p. 11). The note cites evidence that for almost 70% of the tax expenditures in the Global Tax Expenditures Database the revenue estimate is not linked to information on the policy objective (fn. 10, p. 5).

The note is institutional guidance from the IMF's Fiscal Affairs Department, not a peer-reviewed study. It describes a process and a set of questions, and it does not provide a scored checklist. The nine criteria used here (Section 4.3 and Table A1) are therefore the author's operationalisation of the note for document analysis, not an IMF standard.

Why the counterfactual matters is illustrated by the literature on investment incentives, which discusses redundant subsidies and mixed evidence on effects [@zee2002; @vanparys2010; @klemm2012]. @redonda2019 describe tax expenditures as largely opaque and call for more comprehensive reporting. Temporary legislation and sunset clauses are often discussed as review devices [@barsimantov2018].

### 2.4 Transparency, accountability and evaluability

@heald2003 links fiscal transparency to accountability and distinguishes transparency about events from transparency about processes. In this vocabulary, cost reports provide event transparency, whereas evaluation requires process information on objectives, performance and review. Evaluability assessment asks, before an evaluation, whether a programme has defined objectives, a plausible logic and available data; document review is one of its common methods [@trevisan2007; @leviton2010]. This article applies that logic to the official tax expenditure reports: it treats each report as a document and asks whether it contains the information an evaluator would need.

### 2.5 Research on Türkiye

Turkish research on tax expenditures has mainly used the official reports to describe their size and composition. The following characterisations rest on the articles' abstracts, not their full texts. @ericok2019 reviews the concept and the estimates for 2015–2021 and compares tax expenditure ratios across countries. @yoruldu2021 analyses tax expenditures for 2019–2023 in terms of the functions of taxation, using document analysis. @doganalp2020 argues that budget discipline requires analysing both the fiscal cost and the benefits of tax expenditures. @isikman2024 compute deviations between realised and projected tax expenditures in the reports. Comparative work links tax expenditures to fiscal transparency [@dziemianowicz2017] and examines their politics [@haldenwang2021]. As far as these sources show, none of the Turkish studies treats the reports as evaluation instruments, which is the gap addressed here.

## 3. Tax expenditure reporting in Türkiye

Law No. 5018 requires that tax expenditures be shown in the annual budget law. The 2025 report states that this requirement aims to obtain information on the budgetary cost of tax expenditures and on their beneficiaries ("vergi harcamalarından yararlananlarla ilgili olarak bilgi edinilmesi amaçlanmıştır"; VHR2025, PDF p. 23). A first report was prepared in 2001, a report under a new methodology followed in 2007, amounts were published only as budget-law annexes in 2008–2015, and an annual report has been published since 2016 (VHR2025, PDF pp. 12–13). The 2016–2018 reports were issued by the Ministry of Finance (from 2018, the Ministry of Treasury and Finance) and the 2020–2025 reports by the Revenue Administration (Table 1).

**Documents found and not found.** No 2019 report was found in the archives of either institution or under the file names used by the Revenue Administration (Section 4.1). The budget laws for 2019 and 2020 were retrieved from the Official Gazette. Each contains a table titled "Vergi Türleri İtibarıyla Toplam Vergi Harcaması Tahminleri (TL)", which gives totals by tax for three years. The 2019 budget-law table (Official Gazette No. 30642, Mükerrer, 31 December 2018, p. 1973) is identical to the budget-law and projection tables of the 2018 report (178,696 million TL for 2019; VHR2018, PDF pp. 296, 298). It is therefore the same estimate from the same vintage, published twice, and it is counted once. The 2020 budget-law table (No. 30995, Mükerrer, 31 December 2019, p. 1973) (195,591 million TL for 2020) is reproduced in none of the available reports. It is the only published trace of the estimate vintage that the missing 2019 report would have contained. The budget-law annexes also contain a list of the legal bases of tax expenditures, but they give no item-level amounts.

**Structure of the reports.** An introductory chapter defines tax expenditures and the method. A provisions chapter gives one narrative section for each article or group of articles; a section typically quotes the statute and states a rationale, explains why the provision is classified as a tax expenditure, and describes how its cost is to be calculated. A summary chapter presents totals and ratios to GDP and to tax revenue. Annexes give item-level amounts (Annex I), a functional classification (Annex II), the five largest items (Annex III) and their sectoral distribution (Annex IV). Each Annex I row gives one *realisation* year, four *projection* years and an estimate-quality label.

**What "realisation" means.** The annex column headings do not label the first column; its meaning comes from the report text. The 2025 report describes the first annex column as the "gerçekleşme tutarları" (realisation amounts) for 2024 and the remaining columns as projections computed from macroeconomic aggregates (VHR2025, PDF p. 14). It states that the forgone revenues were calculated from 2024 data, while the 2025–2028 amounts were projected on the basis of the macroeconomic targets of the Medium-Term Programme (PDF p. 303). All amounts are revenue-forgone estimates calculated "vergi harcamasının mükellefin davranışında ya da ekonomik aktivitede değişikliğe neden olmayacağı varsayımıyla" (on the assumption that the tax expenditure causes no change in taxpayer behaviour or economic activity; PDF p. 23). A "realisation" figure is therefore a static estimate of revenue forgone based on data for that year, not an observed outcome of the policy. The 2016 report states that its projections were obtained by increasing the 2015 realisation amounts in line with nominal GDP growth (VHR2016, PDF p. 290). Defence-related exemptions and provisions based on international agreements are excluded (VHR2025, PDF p. 23).

**Breaks and changes.** The series has a methodological break in 2016. The 2016 report prints both the old budget-annex series based on the 2007 method (1.34% of GDP for 2015) and the series based on the "yeni belirleme" (new determination; 4.08% of GDP for 2015; VHR2016, PDF pp. 284–290). The two cannot be combined, so the analysis starts in 2016. Coverage also changed. Only the 2016 report lists provisions of stamp duty, fees, inheritance, property and municipal taxes (231 provisions), and it gives no cost estimates for them. Later reports cover seven taxes: personal income tax (GV), corporate tax (KV), VAT (KDV), special consumption tax (ÖTV), banking and insurance transactions tax (BSMV), motor vehicle tax (MTV) and special communication tax (ÖİV). The issuing institution changed between 2018 and 2020. The 2017 report narrates only new provisions. The year emphasised in the summary text also changed: the 2018–2022 reports emphasise the realisation year, whereas the 2023–2025 reports emphasise a projection for the publication year. For example, the 2025 report gives 5.1% of GDP for 2025 in its summary, while its realisation estimate for 2024 is 6.17% (Table 2). Because of these changes, the analysis treats 2016–2018 and 2020–2025 as two reporting regimes and does not present the period as a single continuous series.

## 4. Data and methodology

### 4.1 Documents

Table 1 lists the nine reports analysed. They were downloaded from the issuing institutions' websites on 22 November 2026, and the budget-law annexes were downloaded from the Official Gazette on 23 November 2026. URLs, access dates, file metadata, local copies and the table and page of every value used are recorded in the source inventory (`data/source_inventory.csv`). The search for a 2019 report covered the archive page of the Ministry of Treasury and Finance (reports for 2007 and 2016–2018), the Revenue Administration's report page (2020–2025), the Revenue Administration's file server (the 2019 file name returns HTTP 400), a legacy Revenue Administration file path (HTTP 404) and the Official Gazette. None of these yielded a 2019 report. The 2018 realisation year is therefore missing from the data. Budget-law figures for 2021–2024 are taken from the budget-law tables reprinted in the reports. Those for 2019 and 2020 come from the budget-law annexes themselves, which were published as scanned images and transcribed with optical character recognition and visual checking; their row sums match the printed totals to within 1 TL (`outputs/tables/budget_law_annex_checks.csv`). Because the budget-law annexes contain only totals by tax, they are kept as a separate series (`data/interim/budget_law_annexes.csv`) and are not added to the item-level panel.

### 4.2 Extraction and reconciliation

Annex I tables were extracted programmatically from the PDF files (`scripts/01_extract_annex_tables.py`). The tables of the 2020–2023 reports are printed rotated, and the 2017 report uses a broken character encoding. The 2018 annex uses fonts without Unicode maps; it was decoded with a glyph map learned from the vocabulary of the other reports (`scripts/00_decode_2018_glyphs.py`). Each annex row yields the provision's legal reference, description, five annual amounts with a status code for each cell, and the quality label. Cell statuses distinguish numerical estimates from the reports' standard phrases: "veri temin edilememiştir" (data could not be obtained), "vergi harcaması oluşmamıştır" (no tax expenditure arises), "yeni uygulama" (new measure), "included in another item", and dashes. Five rows of the 2016 report (motor vehicle tax and special communication tax) were transcribed by hand because those pages lack the quality column.

The result is 2,112 report-item rows, one per annex row of each report (`data/interim/items_by_report.csv`), and 10,560 report-item-year observations, five years per row (`data/tax_expenditure_panel.csv`). Two reconciliations were carried out (`scripts/04_reconcile.py`). First, item rows were summed and compared with the printed sub-table totals. They agree within ±5 thousand TL in 395 of the 402 comparisons for which a printed total exists. Second, annex totals were compared with the summary tables in the report bodies. They agree within ±0.5 million TL in every report that publishes such tables, except for two report-years in which the documents themselves are inconsistent (the 2020 report for 2020 and the 2023 report for 2026). Every remaining discrepancy was traced to the documents (`outputs/tables/official_document_inconsistencies.csv`). For example, the printed total of one sub-table in the 2023 report omits a listed item.

Items were linked across reports by legal reference and description similarity (`scripts/02_build_panel.py`), yielding 338 distinct item identifiers. These links are an analytical construct. Comparisons over time use only items linked by both legal reference and description at every step between the reports compared.

Aggregate ratios to GDP and to tax revenue are those published in each report; no denominators are recomputed. Because inflation was high in the period, nominal TL amounts are not compared across years. Amounts for the same fiscal year from different documents are compared, and amount-weighted shares are computed within a single report and year.

### 4.3 Narrative sections and the coding frame

Narrative sections were extracted with page markers: 74 sections in 2016, 22 in 2017, 76 in 2018 and 82–87 in each report from 2020 to 2025, 683 in total (`data/coding/section_codes.csv`). In each report the extracted sections match the table of contents. The 2017 report narrates only new or amended provisions.

Table A1 maps the nine criteria to the elements of the IMF note. Codes take the values 2 (explicit and provision-specific), 1 (partial, generic, shared with other provisions or implicit), 0 (absent from the document) and NA (not applicable). C1 (policy objective), C2 (beneficiaries), C5 (expected outcome), C6 (indicator or outcome data), C7 (additionality or counterfactual), C8 (cost-effectiveness or alternative instruments) and C9 (regular review, sunset clause or renewal evaluation) are coded per narrative section. C3 (item-level cost estimate) and C4 (estimate quality or uncertainty) are coded per item-year from the annex. Two auxiliary variables record whether the estimation method is described (M) and whether the number of beneficiaries is reported (B). The decision rules are in the codebook (`data/codebook.md`).

**Coding procedure and its limits.** Coding proceeded in two layers. First, explicit rules derived from the codebook were applied to every section of every report (`scripts/07_code_sections.py`). The rules separate the report's own narrative from quoted statute text, use cue lists, filter known false cues (for example, purpose words in the titles of laws) and record the evidence sentence and its PDF page for every code. Second, the author reviewed the output. Every C1 and C5 evidence sentence in the 2016 and 2025 reports was read. Every section coded C1 = 0 in those reports was re-read for missed purpose statements. Every rule hit on C6–C8, B and C9 = 2 in any report was examined; all C6, C7 and B hits and all C9 = 2 hits were false positives. Every code that changed between consecutive reports for the same section was checked against both texts. Corrections are logged with a decision identifier (`data/coding/overrides.csv`, `data/coding/decision_log.md`).

All coding was done by one person. No independent second coder was involved, and no inter-coder agreement statistic is reported. The rule layer makes the coding reproducible and auditable, and the review reduces rule errors, but neither is a substitute for independent double coding.

**From sections to items.** Annex items were mapped to narrative sections through their legal references (article number for provisions of the main tax laws, decree number for ÖTV decrees, law number for provisions in other laws; `scripts/09_item_codes.py`). In the 2020–2025 reports 74–84% of items map to a section (Tables 1 and 13). The unmapped items are of two kinds: all BSMV, MTV and ÖİV items, for which the reports contain no narrative sections (24–25 items per report), and items, mostly in the "other laws" sub-tables, whose legal basis has no narrative section (12–40 items per report). Unmapped items are coded 0 on the narrative criteria ("not reported"). In the 2025 report, 28% of the sections to which items map cover more than one item; the largest, on KDV Art. 17 (social, military and other exemptions), covers 25 items. An explicit code for such a section can rest on a purpose stated for only one sub-provision. The section-level codes are therefore reported separately (Table 7), and item-level narrative codes are reported as a range: the *upper bound* attributes the section's code to every mapped item, while the *lower bound* credits an explicit code (2) only to items that are the sole item mapped to their section (Table 11).

## 5. Findings

### 5.1 Scale of reported tax expenditures

Measured against GDP, the realisation-year estimates were about 4.1% in 2015, 2017 and 2019 and 4.70% in 2020. They then rose to 5.85% in 2021 and 6.52% in 2023, and were 6.17% in 2024 (Table 2, Figure 1). Relative to general budget tax revenue, they rose from 19.51% in 2015 to 36.40–38.40% in 2021–2024. The 2015 and 2017 values come from the Ministry-era reports, and the later values from the Revenue Administration reports. No ratio is available for 2016 or 2018. These figures measure the size of support delivered through the tax system under the reports' benchmark; they say nothing about what the support achieves.

[Table 2 — `outputs/tables/table2_official_aggregates.csv`; Figure 1 — `outputs/figures/fig1_te_gdp_vintages.png`]

### 5.2 Revisions between estimate vintages

Table 3 compares estimates for the same fiscal year from different documents. Because each comparison concerns a single year, nominal amounts can be compared directly. In every comparison available, the later realisation estimate exceeded the first projection for that year, by 5.7% to 35.7%. Comparisons with budget-law estimates are more varied. For 2019 the realisation estimate (2020 report) was 0.6% below the budget-law estimate. For 2020 it was 21.4% above. For 2021–2024 it was 24.5% to 162.1% above the budget-law estimates reprinted in the reports. At item level, among items strongly linked between consecutive reports, only 10–24% of realisation estimates fall within ±10% of the earlier projection, and 30–46% differ from it by more than half (Table 3b). These revisions combine macroeconomic forecast errors, inflation, legal changes and updates to data and methods, and the reports do not decompose them. They are revisions of cost estimates, not measures of policy outcomes.

[Table 3 — `outputs/tables/table3_forecast_revisions.csv`; Table 3b — `outputs/tables/table3b_item_revisions.csv`]

### 5.3 Item-level cost information (C3)

Most items carry a numerical estimate for the realisation year (Table 4, Figure 2). Within the Revenue Administration reports, the share of applicable items with an estimate was 79.5% in 2020 and 88.1% in 2025. In the Ministry-era reports it was 72.1–75.5%. Part of the change reflects items entering and leaving the lists. Among items present in both the 2020 and the 2025 report and strongly linked throughout, the share with an estimate was 82.1% in 2020 and 87.2% in 2025 (Table 12).

Gaps remain in every report. Each contains 22–35 items marked "veri temin edilememiştir" (data could not be obtained), and the 2016–2018 reports also contain 10–16 "new measure" items without estimates. Among the 284 linked items that appear in three or more reports, 25 are never estimated and 85 lack an estimate in at least one report (Table 10). Items without an estimate in the 2025 report include the Istanbul Finance Centre exemptions, several corporate tax exemptions for public and village enterprises, and income tax exemptions for trade-fair income.

[Table 4 — `outputs/tables/table4_cost_information.csv`; Figure 2 — `outputs/figures/fig2_cost_information.png`; Table 10 — `outputs/tables/table10_missing_persistence.csv`; Table 12 — `outputs/tables/table12_within_regime_common_items.csv`]

### 5.4 Quality of estimates (C4)

Nearly every estimated item carries a quality label: "çok iyi" (very good), "iyi" (good) or "yaklaşık" (approximate). Between 98% and 100% of estimated items are labelled in each report (Tables 5 and 6). The 2018 and 2020–2025 reports explain the labels in a single sentence: the estimate quality is classified as "çok iyi", "iyi" or "yaklaşık" according to the reliability and coverage of the data used (VHR2025, PDF p. 15). The 2016 report uses a similar sentence, and the 2017 report gives no explanation. No report states the criteria for assigning a label, and none publishes ranges, standard errors or sensitivity analyses. Every labelled item is therefore coded C4 = 1, and none reaches 2.

The use of the labels shifted. In the 2016–2020 reports, items labelled "very good" accounted for 57.8–61.5% of the estimated realisation-year amount. In the 2025 report the share is 14.2%, and items labelled "approximate" account for 42.5% (Table 5, Figure 3). The largest single item in the 2025 report, the exemption of the minimum wage from income tax, accounts for 23.5% of the estimated total and is labelled "approximate" (Table 9). Because the reports do not define the labels, they also do not explain why the labels changed.

[Table 5 — `outputs/tables/table5_estimate_quality.csv`; Figure 3 — `outputs/figures/fig3_estimate_quality.png`]

### 5.5 Objectives and beneficiaries (C1, C2)

*Section level.* In the 2025 report, 51% of applicable narrative sections contain a provision-specific statement of purpose, 23% only a generic or implicit one, and 27% none (Table 7). Examples of specific statements are reducing transport costs to encourage exports and reducing dependence on imported energy while protecting the environment. The most common generic formula, "sosyal ve ekonomik amaç bir arada" (social and economic purposes together) and its variants, appears in 26 of the 84 sections of the 2025 report and in 25 of the 74 sections of the 2016 report (Table 13). Some statements explain an amendment rather than the tax expenditure. In 2025, for example, the section on the exemption for simplified-method taxpayers explains why the exemption was narrowed, not why it exists. Statements can also disappear. The section on the deduction for firms providing services abroad (GVK Art. 33) contained a purpose statement up to the 2021 report, but the shortened text used from 2022 onward does not. Beneficiary groups are almost always defined, usually through the eligibility conditions of the quoted statute (96–97% of sections). No section reports how many taxpayers benefit (B = 0 in all reports; Table 7).

*Item level.* Item-level shares depend on how section codes are attributed to items (Section 4.3). Under the upper bound, 48.1% of items in the 2025 report have an explicit purpose. Under the lower bound, which credits explicit codes only to items that are the sole item of their section, the share is 10.0% (Table 11). Among the 51 one-to-one items, 45.1% have an explicit purpose. Weighted by the 2024 estimated amount, items with an explicit purpose account for 64.8% under the upper bound and 6.2% under the lower bound (Table 11b). Most of the cost is concentrated in multi-provision sections, so the reports allow us to say that a purpose is stated for the article to which an item belongs, but often not for the item itself. Within each reporting regime, the share of strongly linked items with an explicit purpose is stable: 44.3% in 2016 and 41.9% in 2018, and 50.6% in 2020 and 48.9% in 2025 (Table 12).

### 5.6 Outcomes, indicators, additionality and alternatives (C5–C8)

The reports name expected outcomes in general terms but never in measurable ones. In the 2025 report, 37% of applicable sections name an outcome, such as exports, employment, investment, R&D, energy dependence or financial-market depth (43–48% in the 2016 and 2018 reports). No section quantifies an outcome, links it to a target or names an indicator, so C5 never reaches 2 (Tables 6 and 7).

The remaining evaluation criteria are not met in any report. No report provides non-cost data on the use or results of any provision, such as the number of claimants or the investment or employment associated with an incentive (C6 = 0 for all items and sections). No report discusses whether the supported activity would have occurred without the tax expenditure (C7 = 0). No report compares a tax expenditure with an alternative instrument or relates its cost to results (C8). The single exception is a sentence in the sections on the minimum living allowance (*asgari geçim indirimi*) in the 2016–2021 reports, which says that the measure supports wage earners through untaxed revenue "doğrudan kamu harcamaları yapılması yerine" (instead of direct public spending; VHR2016, PDF p. 71); this sentence is coded C8 = 1. These results hold under both attribution bounds and when weighted by cost (Tables 8 and 11b). The ten largest items in the 2025 report account for 62.8% of the 2024 estimated total (Table 9). They include the minimum wage exemption, the exemption for investment funds, the reduced corporate tax rate for investments under incentive certificates and the VAT exemption for feed and fertiliser. Several have stated purposes at section level, but the reports provide no indicator, counterfactual or efficiency information for any of them.

[Table 8 — `outputs/tables/table8_amount_weighted_2025.csv`; Table 9 — `outputs/tables/table9_top10_2025.csv`]

### 5.7 Review and time limits (C9)

About half of the narrative sections reproduce statute text with a time limit or describe a provision as temporary (49–54% of sections in 2016, 2018 and 2025; C9 = 1). An example is the provision of the 2025 report that applies "31/12/2028 tarihine kadar" (until 31 December 2028; VHR2025, PDF p. 104). No section reports a scheduled review, an evaluation underlying a decision to extend or repeal a provision, or the results of a review (C9 = 2 never occurs). The 2016 report itself describes such a function for tax expenditure monitoring. It states that subjecting tax expenditures to budget control makes it possible to determine "düzenleme yapmadaki amacın hala geçerli olup olmadığının" (whether the purpose of a regulation is still valid) and to identify "verimli/verimsiz olan uygulamaların" (efficient and inefficient applications; VHR2016, PDF p. 26). The reports analysed here do not publish information of this kind. Whether such reviews take place outside the reports was not examined.

### 5.8 Description of estimation methods (M)

The narratives say more about how costs should be computed than about what the provisions are expected to achieve. In the 2025 report, 51% of sections describe a provision-specific computation together with a data source, typically a line of the tax return taxed at the statutory or effective rate (M = 2), and 41% describe a computation without a data source (Table 7). The reports caution that where data limitations prevented the ideal method, "uygulanabilir en iyi hesaplama yönteminin kullanılması benimsenmiştir" (the best applicable method was adopted; VHR2025, PDF p. 303). The narrative descriptions are therefore intended computations, not necessarily the computations performed. In the 2025 report, items without a narrative section account for 9.1% of the estimated 2024 amount (Table 13).

## 6. Discussion in relation to the IMF evaluation framework

Table A1 summarises how the reports' content maps onto the IMF note. The reports cover the descriptive steps of the evaluation sequence and stop before the evaluative ones. The first step, "to understand how a TE works, whom it benefits, and its intended purpose" [@beer2022, p. 5], is partly supported. The legal mechanics and eligible groups are reported systematically, a purpose is stated for most articles, and the intended estimation method is described. Item-level revenue-forgone estimates are published for most applicable provisions. Neither element is complete. Purposes are often generic, frequently stated for an article rather than for the specific item, and not formulated in a way that could be tested. The note asks how large and how reliable cost estimates are (Box 2, p. 7). The reports communicate reliability only through labels whose criteria are not disclosed, and they do not report the size of revisions between vintages.

The evaluative elements of the framework have no counterpart in the published reports: indicators defined in advance and a theory of change (pp. 5–6), counterfactual assessment including additionality and displacement (pp. 5, 7), comparison with direct outlays (p. 6), and publication of evaluation results within a periodic cycle (pp. 3–4, 11). This pattern holds in every report from 2016 to 2025, under two issuing institutions, and for the largest items as well as the smallest. It is consistent with the IMF note's cross-country table, which records Türkiye's 2017 reporting as not referring to evaluation [@beer2022, Table 1, p. 3].

Three qualifications apply. First, absence from the reports does not establish absence from government. Evaluations, monitoring data or audits may exist elsewhere, for example in the institutions that administer investment incentive certificates, and this study did not examine such sources. What the analysis shows is that such information, if it exists, is not part of the tax expenditure reporting that accompanies the budget. Second, the IMF note recommends a pragmatic approach that focuses first on the most important tax expenditures and accepts that many evaluations will be qualitative [@beer2022, pp. 4, 12]. The narrative sections, which already describe each provision's mechanics, would be a natural place to add such information. Third, revenue-forgone figures measure gross support under fixed behaviour [@altshuler2011]. The size of revisions between vintages limits how far they can serve as a basis for fiscal decisions. This is a question of the reliability of cost estimates, which the IMF framework treats as part of cost assessment, and is separate from effectiveness.

In terms of the distinction drawn by @heald2003, the reports provide substantial event transparency, showing what is forgone, on which provisions and in which tax. They provide little process transparency about how objectives are set, how performance is judged, and how decisions to extend or repeal provisions are reached. The IMF note warns that without systematic evaluation, the narrative on the benefits of tax expenditures risks being driven by those who benefit from them [@beer2022, p. 1].

## 7. Conclusion and policy implications

Türkiye publishes item-level tax expenditure reports whose annex tables largely reconcile with their own totals. Measured against the information requirements of the IMF evaluation framework, the published reporting makes the cost of tax expenditures visible but does not allow an assessment of their effectiveness. The reports provide item-level revenue-forgone estimates for most applicable provisions, a quality label that is not defined, a definition of eligible groups, and a stated purpose that is often generic or attached to an article rather than to the specific item. They provide no quantified expected outcomes, beneficiary counts, outcome indicators, counterfactual reasoning, efficiency comparisons or review results for any provision in any year. Later estimates for the same year differ substantially from earlier projections and from most budget-law figures, and the reports do not explain these revisions.

The findings suggest incremental changes to the reports, in line with the IMF note's advice to begin with the most important tax expenditures:

1. Define the criteria behind the quality labels, and explain changes in label use between reports.
2. Publish, alongside each budget-law estimate, the later realisation estimate for the same year and the main reasons for the revision.
3. For each provision, starting with the ten largest items (62.8% of the 2024 estimated cost), state a specific objective, an expected outcome and at least one indicator, together with the institution responsible for the data.
4. Examine which administrative sources could provide beneficiary counts for these items, and report counts where the data allow it. This study did not verify which counts are available.
5. Attach a review date to provisions without a statutory time limit, and report the findings of any review in the report.
6. Extend narrative coverage to BSMV, MTV, ÖİV and "other law" provisions, and publish the estimate vintage that was not published as a report (the 2019 vintage appears only as totals in the 2020 budget law).

These recommendations concern information. Whether any particular tax expenditure is effective is a separate question, which can be answered only once such information is available.

## 8. Limitations

The analysis covers the official tax expenditure reports and two budget-law annexes. It does not cover other government sources, such as ministries' statistics or audit reports. The 2019 report was not found, so the 2018 realisation year is missing. Budget-law annexes for years other than 2019 and 2020 were not retrieved separately, and their figures are taken from the tables reprinted in the reports. Statistics of the Ministry of Industry and Technology could not be accessed by automated means; in any case they report planned investment and employment and would not measure costs or effects. Extraction required decoding, and five 2016 rows and the two scanned budget-law tables were transcribed with manual checking; reconciliation against printed totals reduces but cannot exclude transcription errors. Coding was done by a single person using explicit rules and recorded evidence, without an independent second coder. Item-level narrative codes depend on the attribution of section codes to items and are reported as a range. Time limits (C9 = 1) are mostly read from quoted statute and do not show that any review takes place. Revenue-forgone estimates assume no behavioural response and do not measure policy effects or the revenue that repeal would raise. Revisions between vintages are not decomposed into their causes. A fuller list is provided in `paper/limitations_and_data_gaps.md`.
